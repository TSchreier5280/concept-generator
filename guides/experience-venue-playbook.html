Part One

What this does for you

Two cities ran the identical show in the same month. One kept 59 cents of every dollar. The other kept 21. Nothing about the room was different.

The number that matters

Same show, same month, triple the margin

In May 2014, Chicago and Columbus each ran the same sixty-minute production out of the same kit, using the same two manuals. Chicago cleared $14,580 on $24,883. Columbus cleared $1,681 on $7,901.

Read the third and fifth rows together

Columbus sold more transactions than Chicago and served fewer people. It took 397 separate sales to fill 597 seats — one and a half people per sale. Chicago took 272 sales to fill 834 seats — three people per sale.

Columbus was selling seats to individuals at a discount. Chicago was selling rooms to groups at list. The room holds twelve either way. The staffing cost is identical either way. The only thing that changed was who was buying and what they paid.

List price was $28. Chicago collected $29.84 a head. Columbus collected $13.23 — under half. The customer survey opens by asking "How did you hear about us?" and the first option printed on the page is Groupon / Living Social / daily deal site. That is where the margin went.

What you are actually buying

Four assets, not one idea

The reason this ran in sixteen markets at once is that none of it depended on the founder being in the room. It was four transferable assets:

  • A kit that shipsThe entire set moves in a single POD — furniture, clues, locks, timer, signage. A new market opens by unloading a container, not by commissioning a build.
  • Two manualsOne for the cast, one for the local producer. Between them they cover hiring, training, scheduling, invoicing, refunds, emergencies, minors, and what to do when only one actor shows up.
  • Fixed unit economics$25 a show for the host, $30 for the performer, $600 a month for the local producer. Every market's cost line is knowable before it opens.
  • A scarcity-built booking systemTickets release exactly one month ahead, at 12:30 in the morning. Nothing further out is purchasable — which is what pushes buyers into groups.

Part Two

How to run it

The show is sixty minutes. The operation is a two-hour-twenty turn, and it repeats. Everything below is timed off the clock, because that is how the room was actually run.

The turn

One cycle, start to reset

Two people run the entire operation. There is no third role, no manager on duty, no front-of-house staff. The times below are the published run sheet for an evening slot.

5:50
Cast arrives, performer disappears

Costume and makeup in under ten minutes. The performer must be inside the room and out of sight before the first guest walks in — the reveal is the product.

6:10
Guests arrive — four things, in order

Coats hung. Waiver signed — every guest, no exceptions. Bathroom, framed as a joke so nobody is embarrassed to go. Name tag, and they can be anyone they want. The host writes every name down; that list becomes the closing script.

6:30
Rules, tips, questions

Delivered standing at the posted signs, not recited from memory. Any question about what happens inside gets one answer: "You'll find out."

6:45
Doors close, clock starts

Hard deadline. Latecomers past the fifteen-minute mark are rescheduled, not squeezed in — a late entry changes the dynamics of the room for everyone already inside.

7:45
Buzzer, applause, bow

Called loud and immediately, win or lose. The room applauds the performer by name. The performer bows, breaks character, and starts the reset while the guests are still in the room.

7:47
Curtain call — the part that sells the next ticket

Every guest is named for one specific thing they did. Then photos with the signs, a Polaroid taped to the wall, and a flyer in hand. Detail below.

8:00
Group leaves, room is already reset

The performer's last act is recoiling the chain, because it is loud. By then the guests should be gone.

8:10
Next group arrives

Ten minutes of slack in a two-hour-twenty cycle. That is the entire buffer, which is why the reset is choreographed rather than tidied.

The mechanism

Two roles, secretly one

Host / Omnipotent

$25 per show

  • Before: checks in, collects waivers, makes small talk, writes down every name.
  • During: silent. Guides with gesture and stare only. Holds the exit key on a clipboard at all times.
  • After: becomes the host again and runs the curtain call from the name list.

The Performer

$30 per show

  • Tracks the puzzle state and physically blocks or opens access to the clue the group needs next.
  • Never stands, never chases. The threat of contact does the work; a chase is a safety problem.
  • Keeps the tension line taut — the constraint is the antagonist, not the costume.

Unknown to the audience, both roles are working the same objective: get this group to the final step with about a minute left. Not to beat them, and not to let them coast. Every group should walk out believing they were thirty seconds from the other outcome.

That is a manufactured result, produced live, every hour, by two contractors earning $55 between them. It is also the only reason the curtain call works.

The retention engine

The curtain call is not a courtesy

Most groups do not escape. Left alone, they leave feeling stupid, and they do not refer anyone. The curtain call exists to convert a loss into a story worth repeating, and it is scripted:

  • Name every person for something specific. Who found the hidden clue, who said the funniest line, who kept notes. This is what the name list was for.
  • Rank them against other groups, favourably. "Most groups take the whole hour to get that — you had it in ten minutes." People who lose immediately want to know how they compare.
  • Photos with the win sign or the loss sign. Both signs exist. Losing is given its own trophy.
  • Ask for the referral in the room. Escaped: "Are your friends as smart as you are?" Didn't: "Invite your friends and see if they do better." Flyer in hand as they say it.
  • Protect the secret. Share anything online, but no clues — that is what keeps the room resellable.
  • Sell the return visit. They cannot play again knowing the answers. They can come back for $10 as the silent guide and watch their friends suffer.

Note what that last one is: a $10 ticket that costs nothing to fulfil, sold to someone who has already paid once, whose whole job is to bring a new full-price group with them.

Opening a market

Three and a half days to open

The Los Angeles opening report is the template, and it is short because the process is genuinely this compressed:

D1
Licence, container, build

Business licence filed at city hall that morning and framed by the door. Container unloaded the same afternoon; set repaired and staged into the night.

D2
Audition show — thirteen candidates

Not an audition room. A real performance, run for the candidates, so they see the job before they take it. Set faults found here get fixed the same night.

D3
Second audition show, lobby furnished, producer hired

Ten more candidates. Lobby furnished from a yard sale and a hardware store the same afternoon. The local producer is offered the job that evening.

D4
Producer trained, cast trained, team formed

Producer walked through keys, repairs, scheduling, guest email and paperwork. Two training shows for the cast. The team resets the room together twice, rehearses the difficult scenarios — a group with no reservation, a drunk group, a group that simply is not getting it — and agrees a safe word.

Twenty-eight people auditioned across three shows. Five were invited onto the opening team and four accepted. Everyone else was called personally and put on a ranked understudy list before the trainer left town. A market that opens with four contractors and no bench closes the first time somebody gets the flu.

What the first opening taught them

The report's own lessons list is the most valuable page in the archive, because it is all failure: furniture shipped unsecured arrived scratched and broken and cost three and a half hours to repair; clues packed loose inside the furniture damaged it in transit and took hours to find; fixtures came loose and nobody knew what belonged to what; a cabinet arrived with mould on it; three separate clues were printed wrong and shipped anyway; and the contractor agreement referred to the signer as an employee rather than a contractor.

Every one of those became a packing rule. That is what a playbook is — somebody else's bad weekend, written down so it is not yours.

Non-negotiable

The rules that are not preferences

  • No waiver, no entry.Stated twice in both manuals, in capitals. There is no version of this that is worth the exception.
  • The exit key never leaves the room.It stays on the guide's clipboard. Anyone who wants out gets out, immediately, no negotiation. A child leaving must be accompanied by an adult.
  • Injury stops the show.Character breaks, help is found. Nothing else is being weighed.
  • Never move the clues.The guide's entire performance depends on knowing where everything is. A "helpful" rearrangement wrecks the next six shows.
  • Contact is theatre, never force.Only a cornered guest, only slowly, never mid-movement. Nobody is chased.
  • Refund the unhappy guest.The policy says no refunds. The manual says pay them anyway. A $28 refund is cheaper than the review.
  • The room is reset for tomorrow before anyone goes home.Not for tonight. For the first show of the next day, when someone may be running late.

If you take one thing

Sell rooms, not seats

Your cast cost, your rent, and your hour are fixed the moment you open the doors. The only variable you control is who is standing in the room and what they paid to be there.

Chicago and Columbus prove it inside a single month, with no marketing spend on either side. The difference between a 59% margin and a 21% margin was not a better room, a better cast, or a better city. It was that one market sold twelve seats at once to a group who wanted the whole room, and the other sold them one and a half at a time to people holding a coupon.

Everything else in this playbook — the scarcity window, the curtain call, the referral ask, the $10 return ticket, the ranked understudy bench — exists to make the first thing happen and the second thing unnecessary.
