Second draft Johnstown, Colorado 9 runs a year

What changed

Eight holes in the first draft

Two of them were serious enough to have stopped the business in week one. They are listed here rather than quietly patched, because the fixes reshaped the plan.

  • The show was not ours to tourThe first draft assumed we could rebrand the original production. The format — a constrained performer, a timed release, a silent guide — is not protectable. The script, the characters, the puzzle designs and the name absolutely are, and the contractor agreements in the archive included a non-compete. Fix: an original property, built below.
  • Escape rooms are a regulated occupancyUnder NFPA 101 and IBC/IFC Section 411, a room like this is a special amusement building — it takes assembly-occupancy provisions regardless of occupant load, plus smoke detection under reduced lighting and 90 minutes of emergency illumination. Putting it in an empty retail bay is a change of occupancy that needs a new certificate whether or not you touch the walls. Fix: the venue order is inverted, below.
  • Sales tax was missing entirelyJohnstown's combined rate is roughly 7.9%. Colorado also runs a special-event sales tax licence, which is the right instrument for a touring show. Not income, but not optional either.
  • Nobody was selling the corporate bookingsThe whole model turns on ten corporate slots per run, and the first draft had no one selling them and no cost for doing so. A booking commission is now in the P&L.
  • Merchandise and photos were left on the tableThe original's own guest survey asked what merchandise people would buy, and its ledgers show t-shirt sales. At 432 guests a run this is real money at near-zero marginal cost.
  • No working-capital figureAnnual profit is not the question. How deep the hole goes before the first ticket sells is the question.
  • Estes Park contradicted the modelA walk-up tourist market has no corporate base and cannot be sold in buyouts — it is structurally the weak column. Repriced and re-scoped.
  • Insurance was under-costed$400 a run for a show involving physical performers and confined guests was optimistic. Now an annual policy, allocated.

The show

The Fall — Johnstown, 1924

On 6 July 1924, roughly two hundred people at a graveside funeral outside Johnstown watched something come out of the sky and bury itself four feet into the ground. Being at a burial, they had shovels. They dug it up.

You do not need to license anybody's zombie. Johnstown has its own legend, it is true, it is out of copyright, and it is better.

The Johnstown meteorite fell during a funeral service at the Dilley Chapel in Elwell, two miles west of the present town. Twenty-seven fragments were eventually recovered across a fall zone stretching roughly ten miles south to Mead. The largest piece — about fifty-two pounds — sits in the American Museum of Natural History in New York. A dime-sized piece is on display at the Historic Parish House and Museum on Charlotte Street, and the town threw the rock a hundredth birthday party in 2024.

The premise: you are the mourners. The service is interrupted. The thing is on the table in the back room of the chapel, still warm, and the hour you have is the hour before the county men arrive to take it away. Something came down with it.

Handle the history carefully

The 1924 funeral was for a real man from Milliken, and his descendants are plausibly still in the area. The show invents its own deceased and says so plainly in the programme. The Johnstown Historical Society is a partner worth more than any plot point — do not make a monster out of somebody's great-grandfather.

Why this property, specifically

  • You own it outright. No licence, no royalty, no non-compete, no phone call you would rather not make.
  • The fall zone is the touring route. Elwell, Johnstown, Milliken, Mead — a ten-mile scatter that maps almost exactly onto the first four stops. Every town on the line can legitimately claim a piece of the story, and the marketing writes itself: it landed here too.
  • It buys you the press for free. A local historical event with a museum, a centenary and an MIT connection gets covered. A generic haunt does not.
  • It is a partnership, not a competitor. The Historical Society and the Parish House Museum gain a reason for people under forty to care about their collection. Give them a cut of the door or a night of their own.
  • It scales the right way. Year two, every new market gets its own local legend on the same chassis. The mechanism travels; the story is always theirs.

The engine, unchanged

What carries over from the archive is not the story — it is the machine underneath it. One performer, physically constrained, whose reach extends by one increment every time a bell sounds. Twelve guests, sixty minutes, five sequential discoveries. A silent guide who never speaks and never stops steering.

Both operators are secretly working the same objective, and it is not to win:

The reframe

Stop paying rent to wait for customers

The original paid $860 to $1,000 a month per city for a room that sat dark most of the week. Going pop-up deletes that line — and hands you three different problems.

  • Rent becomes variableYou pay a venue only for nights you are selling. In a permanent room every dark Tuesday is a cost. On a run there are no dark Tuesdays — you are not there.
  • Scarcity becomes realThe original faked urgency by releasing tickets exactly one month out at 12:30 in the morning. "Nine nights in Johnstown, then it's gone" needs no trick. It is simply true.
  • Reputation stops accruing to a location — this is the billA permanent room compounds reviews, search ranking and walk-past traffic for years. You rebuild awareness every run. The list you take with you is the only asset that compounds, which is why one brand, one ticketing site and one mailing list across every market is non-negotiable.
  • And you re-permit every stop — this is the other billNine markets is nine fire marshals. Solved below, but never free.

The original booked $0 marketing in both Chicago and Columbus, because a room that has been open eighteen months markets itself. You do not have that luxury.

The market

Johnstown is not the market. It is the address.

23,265 people cannot fill 42 shows. But a town twenty minutes from half a million of them, in the fastest-growing corridor in Colorado, is an excellent place to keep a trailer.

Why this town

Johnstown was the third-fastest-growing municipality in Colorado over 20,000 last year, in the county that led the state. It sits on I-25 at the Highway 34 and Highway 60 interchanges, which is why Buc-ee's built there and why the town keeps drawing national "boomtown" coverage. New arrivals have no established weekend habits. And a town growing 3.7% a year has commercial space coming online faster than it fills.

Compliance

The fire marshal decides this business, not the landlord

This was the first draft's worst mistake. Get it right and it becomes a moat — most people who try this will not read the code.

A room where people are confined and solve puzzles is a special amusement building under NFPA 101 and IBC/IFC Section 411. It takes assembly-occupancy provisions regardless of how few people are inside. Twelve guests does not exempt you.

That means, at minimum: an approved automatic smoke detection system if you operate under reduced lighting; emergency illumination along every egress path good for ninety minutes; and exits that never require a tool, a key, or the solving of a puzzle to open. Any electronic lock has to release from the egress side and fail open on power loss.

Dropping this into a vacant retail bay is a change of occupancy — retail to assembly — which triggers a new certificate of occupancy whether or not you touch a single wall. That is not a three-week conversation. Repeated nine times a year, it is the business.

Which is why the venue order inverts

The first draft led with vacant retail. Wrong. Lead with rooms that are already classified for assembly, already sprinklered, already inspected, already carrying a certificate. You are then a temporary event inside a compliant building rather than an occupancy change.

First call — already compliant

Event & wedding venues

Over a hundred are listed around Johnstown. Already assembly-rated with egress, sprinklers and a fire file. Every one has dead Thursday inventory it will never sell. Revenue share beats a flat fee — they lose nothing by saying yes.

Second call — already compliant

Brewery taprooms & halls

Local taprooms run private events to around 75. Smaller footprint, but assembly-classified, and they keep the bar while you keep the door. Their mailing list becomes your marketing at no cost.

Third call — needs work

Vacant retail bay

Ledge Rock Center and 2534 have unleased space and landlords who want foot traffic. Genuinely cheap — but budget for a temporary-use permit, a fire official walkthrough, and possibly a change-of-occupancy review. Only worth it for a long or repeat run.

The beachhead

BBQ Day, first Saturday in June

A century-old town festival in Parish Park with a formal sponsorship programme, parade and car show. Do not run the show. Run a fifteen-minute outdoor taster, collect emails all day, sell the September run off that list.

Turn the constraint into the pitch

Design the show so the door is never locked — the guide holds the release, anyone can leave at any second, and there is no puzzle between a guest and the exit. The original already worked this way with a key on the guide's clipboard. Now say it out loud in the marketing. "You are never locked in" removes the single biggest objection from claustrophobic guests, nervous parents and corporate HR — and it happens to be exactly what the code demands.

The circuit

Nine runs, one trailer

Two days in, nine nights across three weekends, one day out. About twenty-four days a market, then twenty minutes down the road to a market that has never seen it.

Come back with a different show, not the same one

The original never solved repeat business; its answer was a $10 ticket to return and watch your friends, which is clever but small. A circuit solves it properly — a market that saw the show in September is a warm list in June, provided what you bring back is new. The original ran a second production in two of its cities for exactly this reason. Build show two in year two.

Two things this table now says that the first draft could not

The merchandise table pays for the booker and the permits. Adding photos and shirts brought in $3,390 in the mixed case; the booking commission, cost of goods and permits together took out $2,840. The ancillary line funds the entire back office the first draft had forgotten to staff, and leaves $550 on top.

Merchandise narrows the gap between the columns but does not close it. The weak column improved from an $11,000 year to a $21,000 one, because photos sell per head and public shows still have heads. It is still less than a third of the mixed case. Selling seats instead of rooms is survivable now. It is still the wrong business.

In May 2014 the original ran the identical production in two cities in the same month. Chicago cleared 58.6%. Columbus cleared 21.3% — taking more transactions to serve fewer people, 1.5 heads per sale against Chicago's 3.07, because it sold discounted seats to individuals while Chicago sold whole rooms to groups. Twelve years and a trailer later it is still the only number that decides the outcome: sell rooms, not seats.

The number that actually matters is the trough

The build is spent across roughly three months with no revenue behind it. Corporate deposits are the only thing arriving early — ten slots at 50% down is $3,125 before load-in. Add one run's operating float of about $15,600 and peak cash requirement lands near $36,000 at the low build and $52,000 at the high one, somewhere around week ten.

At the mixed case each run returns $7,204, so cumulative breakeven falls between the fifth and eighth run — Windsor in February at best, Johnstown's June return at worst. Build cheap. Every dollar saved on the set is a month earlier to breakeven, and the set is the one line where a dollar saved costs nothing the audience can see.

For comparison: a permanent escape-room build-out in this market starts around $50,000 and runs well past six figures once a lease is signed. The trailer is the cheap half of the idea.

The playbook

What carries over, and what has to be rebuilt

Most of the archive survives the move to pop-up untouched. A short list does not, and those are the ones that will hurt.

Carries over unchanged

  • The 2h20 turnGuests in at :10, doors close at :45, sixty-minute show, curtain call, room reset while the group is still inside, next group at :10. Ten minutes of slack, which is why the reset is choreographed rather than tidied.
  • Two roles, secretly oneA silent guide and a constrained performer, both working the hidden objective in the diagram above. $60 and $75 a show.
  • The curtain callName every guest for something specific, rank them favourably against past groups, photos with both the win sign and the loss sign, referral asked for in the room. Most groups do not escape; this is what converts a loss into a story worth repeating.
  • The non-negotiablesNo waiver, no entry. The release never leaves the guide's hand. Injury stops the show. Never move the clues. Contact is theatre, never force. Refund the unhappy guest regardless of policy.
  • The audition-show hireOpen a market by running the real show for candidates, hire four, and call everyone else personally onto a ranked understudy bench before you leave town.

Has to be rebuilt

  • The story, from scratch and owned outrightThe Fall, above. The mechanism is a format; the script, characters and puzzles have to be ours.
  • The packing rules become the weekly disciplineThe original's Los Angeles opening report is a catalogue of one bad weekend — furniture shipped unsecured and broken, clues packed loose inside the furniture, fixtures nobody could match, mould on a cabinet, three clues printed wrong and shipped anyway. That was a one-time embarrassment for a permanent install. You load out nine times a year. That list is now the checklist, and it is the most valuable page in the archive.
  • Cast: a touring core plus a local benchTwo travelling performers hold quality across markets. Run the audition show in each new town anyway and build a local understudy list — illness twenty minutes from home is survivable, illness in Estes Park is not.
  • One brand, one site, one list, every runReviews and ranking have to accrue somewhere. Give each market its own page and you start from zero nine times a year. The show is the brand; the town is this month's dateline.
  • A compliance pack that travelsCertificate of insurance, waivers, life-safety plan, egress diagram, special-event tax licence, and last month's fire sign-off in one folder. Walk into every jurisdiction with the answers already printed. This is also what makes a landlord say yes in one meeting instead of three.
  • Sell the run before you open itCorporate and buyout bookings close before load-in, not during. A room sold in week three at a discount is worth less than the same room sold in advance at list, and the cast costs the same either way.

Weeks
1–2
Ask the fire marshal before you ask the landlord

One conversation with the Johnstown building department and the fire authority, describing the show honestly, before a dollar is committed. If the answer is that every stop needs a change-of-occupancy review, the venue strategy changes and you have spent nothing finding out. This is the cheapest question in the whole plan and the first draft did not ask it.

Lock the story and the partner

Sit down with the Johnstown Historical Society and the Parish House Museum. Bring them in as a partner with a stake, not as a footnote — their blessing is what turns a haunt into a local event, and their objection is what turns it into a problem.

Honestly

What could still sink this

  • A jurisdiction says no. Everything rests on being a temporary event inside an already-compliant building. If a fire authority insists on a full change-of-occupancy review per stop, the circuit slows to three or four runs a year and the economics halve. Test it in week one.
  • The weak column is the default, not the worst case. Selling individual tickets is easier than selling buyouts, so it is what happens if nobody is deliberately pushing the other way. Twenty-one thousand a year for full-time work.
  • Northern Colorado already has escape rooms. Fort Collins, Loveland and Greeley each have several established operators with years of reviews. The defence is that this is a live performance on a limited run with a story the town already half-knows — but that has to be obvious in every piece of marketing, or you are a more expensive version of what is already there.
  • Nine load-ins a year is physically hard. The model assumes the same two people tour all year. Build the local bench early and treat burnout as a scheduling problem, not a character flaw.
  • The story could be too small to travel. The meteorite is a gift in Johnstown, Milliken and Mead. In Longmont it is a curiosity. Watch the March numbers closely — that stop exists to answer this question.
